An education in the age of AI

What I learned as a master's student in the education age of AI — about anxiety, economics, and the one thing the machines couldn't do for me.

I want to tell you about the most productive moment of my education. It didn't happen in a classroom, and it didn't feel productive. It happened during the busiest quarter of my life — three courses, including the one students warn each other about, and two research jobs — when a thought started following me home at night: machines are getting cheap at exactly the thing I am working this hard to become. Every hour I spent training my mind, some datacenter was training its replacement — or so the feeling went. I suspect everyone in this generation of students has met some version of that thought. Most of us do the sensible thing and push it away.

I couldn't, because of a habit economics gave me: an economist treats anxiety as a modeling assignment. If the fear is real, it has parameters. If it has parameters, you can study it instead of carrying it.

I should say how I got here, because the road matters to the lesson. I came from Vietnam to study economics in Canada, and the first real thing I ever built was an immigration chatbot — because I had been the person who needed one. Economics taught me that every honest claim is a comparison, but my questions kept demanding more computation than my training could supply. So I went looking, and I found something beautiful waiting on the other side. Statistics had always carried a dream — flexible models, rich with parameters, that would let the data finally speak — and for a century that dream was starved, because the computation didn't exist. Then it did. That's what machine learning is: an old dream that suddenly got an engine. I fell for it completely, and I thought I was leaving economics behind. In truth, economics was just waiting for a better question.

The question found me in that overloaded spring, and I gave it the only answer I knew how to give: I scheduled time for it. Literal calendar blocks, in my worst quarter, to build an economic model of whether human work becomes worthless or priceless when machine thinking costs as much as electricity — and then to turn that model into a story anyone with the same knot in their stomach could scroll through on a phone. That story was judged blind, by people who knew nothing about me, and it won.

But here is the part I didn't plan: while I was building a model about the value of human work, my own research was quietly collecting the evidence. In the lab, I used AI every day. It accelerated the code, the experiments, the searching — honestly, everything. Everything except the part that mattered most. When months of results had to become one coherent argument that could convince skeptical reviewers, the machine had nothing to offer but fluency. It could answer any question I asked precisely — and only if I knew precisely what to ask. It could write pages, and it could not own a single one of them. The thinking, the judgment of what matters, the responsibility for the claim — those stayed stubbornly, reassuringly human.

There's a name for why, and I ended up citing it in the piece itself. The Berkeley computer scientist Michael I. Jordan likes to point out that a market that feeds a city of millions every day — with no single mind planning it — is itself a kind of intelligence. Intelligence was never a thing that lived inside one head, and it does not live inside one black box now. It lives between us. Science works exactly this way: my paper does not become knowledge when the model finishes running. It becomes knowledge when a skeptical reviewer — a stranger who owes me nothing — reads months of my reasoning, tests my methodology against the gap in the literature I claim it fills, and is convinced. That loop — thinking, arguing, being believed — is the intelligence. The machine accelerated my experiments, and I'm glad it did. But acceleration was never the argument. What doesn't survive the reviewer is noise.

So the model said it, my life confirmed it, and now I'll say it to you plainly. Einstein had to hunt for knowledge — through libraries, through letters, through the work of other experts. For most of history, access to knowledge was the bottleneck of genius. Your generation inherits the opposite condition: AI is the greatest dictionary ever compiled, and it has made knowledge nearly free. But no dictionary has ever written a sentence. The facts are no longer the prize; the machines have the facts. What's becoming scarce — and therefore, as any economist will tell you, what's becoming valuable — is the trained mind that can interpret what the dictionary holds, put it together, and explain it so another human being actually understands. That is precisely the thing an education, done right, builds. Not the notes you'll forget. The cognition you'll keep. In an age of information abundance, we don't need fewer trained minds — we need more of them than we have ever needed, because someone has to turn all that free knowledge into meaning.

Two centuries ago, machines learned to weave, and the weavers never recovered. The same century, machines learned to make portraits — and painting flourished, because the world decided that “made by a human” was worth paying for. Same shock; opposite endings; the difference was a choice. That choice is in front of us again, and it will be made by the people in rooms like this one. So the future I'd leave you with isn't a forecast, because forecasts are things you obey. It's an equilibrium — and an equilibrium is something you design.

  2. July 2026

    “Will AI Make Human Work Worthless — or Priceless?” won the Graduate Division of the CAUSE Student Data Scrollytelling Contest — a nine-act interactive data story in D3.js and Scrollama.js, built around an original nested-CES general-equilibrium model of AI and cognitive labor.   3. July 2026

    Found the geometric signature behind the encoder effect: the same fine-tuning recipe collapses the held-out effective rank of the three out-of-domain encoders' embeddings (10.5 to 1.1, 9.1 to 2.4, 20.2 to 2.5) while expanding the mammography-native encoder's from 7.2 to 13.3. Collapse versus expansion separates the encoders whose training-set class separation carries over to held-out data from those where it does not.

  4. July 2026

    Tested whether the effect was specific to the label the encoder was fine-tuned on by scoring the untrained, finer five-class BI-RADS category instead of the three-class suspicion label. The gain held on both retrieval-sensitive decoders (accuracy +23.2% and +14.6%; quadratic weighted kappa 0.100 to 0.302), though nearly all of it landed on the majority BI-RADS categories rather than the clinically decisive ones.

  5. July 2026

    Added a fourth encoder, BioCLIP (organism / tree-of-life images), which shares the other out-of-domain encoders' architecture and fine-tuning schedule but not their pretraining domain. That completed the design: four retrieval-encoder substrates, each evaluated frozen and fine-tuned, crossed with three frozen VLM decoders — 24 evaluation cells. Began drafting the paper in the PSB conference format.

  6. July 2026

    Confirmed the headline result. Fine-tuning the mammography-native encoder (Mammo-CLIP) produced the only arm to clear the held-out majority-class retrieval floor of P@1 = 0.487, moving from 0.513 frozen to 0.585 fine-tuned; the same recipe applied to the generic web-image and biomedical-figure encoders inflated training-set separation but left held-out retrieval tied with that floor. The retrieval gain propagated into the generated reports on the two retrieval-sensitive decoders (suspicion accuracy +20.7% and +15.6% relative to the frozen baseline), while MedGemma did not respond to it. Wrote up the full analysis in a 37-page technical report for my advisor, Dr. Wenjing Yang.

  7. May 2026

    Fine-tuned the retrieval encoder for the first time and found a warning sign: its training-set class separation grew far more than its held-out separation. That overfitting signal is what motivated testing the same fine-tuning recipe on encoders pretrained on different image domains.

  8. April 2026

    On a second research position, with Dr. Brian Fischer, designed a 7-table normalized MySQL schema (owl, recording session, neuron, experiment, tuning parameters, spike reliability, ILD conditions tested) for his lab's barn owl auditory-neuroscience recordings — roughly 14,000 raw files in six proprietary formats, 110 neurons across 8 owls, about 261 experiments — and built the 6-phase ETL pipeline that loads it. An analysis loop that previously required 228 individual file loads now runs as a single SQL query.

  9. March 2026

    Began a critical replication of a published retrieval-augmented mammography report-generation pipeline: explored the CBIS-DDSM dataset first, then switched to VinDr-Mammo (5,000 studies) to match the paper's own dataset, and set up evaluation of three vision-language model decoders (MedGemma, Qwen2.5-VL, LLaVA-Med) across several prompting conditions using the paper's own frozen retrieval encoder.
